Overview

As Lead Engineer, I ran the full development of a tactical small counter-UAS (CUAS) interceptor for Canadian defence missions, from the first concept to a working prototype. The drone was built as a prototype to compete in Canada's Counter-UAS Sandbox program and was designed for speeds of about 300 km/h. Drone threats change almost daily, so the design focused on low cost, easy field repair, and the ability to mass-produce in weeks rather than months. I used rapid prototyping, mainly 3D printing, to go from design to test hardware quickly and to make changes easily as requirements evolved. The result is a domestically designed and built airframe that can be produced, repaired and upgraded quickly.

Printed airframe hub

The airframe was split into four sections, each sized to print in one piece on the Bambu Lab X1-Carbon's 256 × 256 × 256 mm build plate:

  • Nose section (PETG), which houses the camera for target acquisition
  • Forward fuselage (PETG)
  • Aft fuselage (CF-nylon), which carries the wings and motors
  • Tail section (PETG)

The cone shape required a vertically stacked avionics column, with the flight controller, ESCs, power distribution and communication systems layered in a tight space. The wiring order was planned so every connection stayed reachable as each layer went in.

Everything comes together in one balanced configuration: the camera in the nose, motors on the wings, and battery management and propulsion integrated through the core.

Manufacturing

CF-nylon sections were printed with a hardened-steel nozzle in the X1-Carbon's enclosed chamber, with the filament dried before each print.

Layer height, print speed, wall count, infill and supports were tuned in the slicer, over several rounds, until the surface was smooth enough for high-speed flight.

Heat-set threaded inserts, pressed into the printed parts with a soldering iron, give reusable bolted joints between sections and hard mounting points for the avionics. A damaged section can be reprinted and swapped without rebuilding the airframe.

Overview

An indigenous Medium-Altitude Long-Endurance (MALE) aircraft developed jointly by Bharat Electronics Limited (BEL) and the Aeronautical Development Establishment (ADE) of DRDO, for Intelligence, Surveillance, Target Acquisition and Reconnaissance (ISTAR) missions for the Indian armed forces.

Mission profile

Intelligence, Surveillance, Target Acquisition and Reconnaissance (ISTAR) as well as armed operational roles.

My role and highlights

  • Designed the airframe and Led the structural layout, assembly design and released documentation in 3DEXPERIENCE and CATIA V5, version-controlled through ENOVIA.
  • Optimized airframe structures for weight, strength and manufacturability, taking designs from concept to production-ready drawings.
  • Designed the installation of aircraft systems with the stress, aerodynamics, propulsion, avionics, electrical and landing gear teams, resolving interfaces between those systems and the structure.

Airframe construction

  • Hybrid structure: carbon-fibre composites form the main load-carrying structure and skins, and metallic parts are used where loads are concentrated or fire protection is needed.
  • Wing skins, strakes and fuselage shell: CFRP foam-sandwich construction, giving high stiffness for low weight.
  • Wing spars and ribs: monolithic (solid) CFRP laminates that carry the main bending and shear loads.
  • Control surfaces: CFRP with a foam core, for lightweight, stiff moving surfaces.
  • Bulkheads and longerons: machined aluminium alloys at the major load paths.
  • Equipment frames: bolted aluminium assemblies.

Composite wing spar

  • A domestically developed CFRP sandwich spar for the canard-configuration main spar (load carrying member), designed for strength, stiffness and producibility.
  • It delivered a 15% gain in structural efficiency and a 10% weight reduction over the baseline design.
  • The design was taken through to production with design-for-manufacture drawings, tooling requirements for moulding, and an engineering bill of materials (EBOM) kept accurate through every design change.

3D design and 2D manufacturing drawings

  • 3D models of parts and assemblies were built in 3DEXPERIENCE and CATIA V5, covering the structural layout, assembly sequence and system installation interfaces.
  • Detailed 2D part and assembly drawings with GD&T (geometric dimensioning and tolerancing) define the parts, processes, build sequence and special instructions for the shop floor.
  • Supplementary planning documents and build-sequence definitions were written to make production more efficient and reduce errors traced through engineering change reports.
  • All data is version-controlled in ENOVIA. Drawings, work instructions, process specifications and compliance documents were released through a formal engineering change process (ECR/ECO/PCR) that assessed each change's impact on design, cost and schedule.

Integral composite fuel tank

  • The fuel tanks are built into the wing strake composite structure, so the aircraft structure itself holds the fuel. This saves weight and increases fuel volume for long endurance.
  • Tank boundaries, joints and access provisions were designed around sealing and leak prevention.
  • Colour-coded sealing model: a zero-thickness, colour-coded surface model was built on the wing strake CAD model, going beyond the standard 2D drawing set. It gave the manufacturing team a 3D visual map of every surface treatment:
    • Primer: surface preparation, so the sealant bonds firmly and lastingly to the composite.
    • Class A (brushable) sealant: applied along all fuel faying surfaces.
    • Class B (paste) sealant: applied to corners, fillets, fasteners and overlapping joints, closing every path fuel could escape through.
    • Structural bonding surfaces: where the composite ribs join the upper and lower skins.
  • Step-by-step assembly procedure: each step is linked to the colour-coded model, from surface preparation through priming, sealing and final bonding. The sequence is set up so that no step can be done out of order without the missing earlier step being visible on the model.
  • Result: the first leak test passed with zero leaks across the whole tank assembly, and no rework was needed.

Overview

A loitering munition is a single-use precision strike system that stays under operator control until the moment of impact. These systems are designed to be man-portable, short-range, fast, and stealthy. They suit short missions, so they need little fuel and carry small warheads that hit targets accurately while limiting collateral damage.

Because they are expendable, loitering munitions cost far less than bomber aircraft or missiles. They are also quick to deploy and need few operators and little maintenance. Paired with lightweight modern avionics, they can operate autonomously without human input.

Their low radar cross-section (RCS), along with small visual and thermal signatures, makes loitering munitions difficult to detect and intercept. On future battlefields, the side that uses them most effectively may well gain the decisive advantage.

Sub systems

The overall system is catagorized into three subsystems as following:

  1. EO/IR system
  2. Autonoumous system
  3. Propulsion system
  4. Ground control station (GCS)
  5. Launcher system
  6. Payload system

Mission profile

  1. Stage 1LaunchThe loiter munition UAV will be launched via a portable Tube launcher.
  2. Stage 2ClimbThis involves low signature climb of the UAV to a predetermined altitude (Autonomous).
  3. Stage 3CruiseSemi-autonomous and target check (detection). Image processing and target geo-location
  4. Stage 4LoiterThe UAV loiters around the target at an altitude where it can perform recognition and to gather additional data.
  5. Stage 5DescentThe UAV descends to an altitude where it can identify the target.
  6. Stage 6AttackOn confirmation, the UAV executes a high-speed dive and destroys the target.

01EO/IR system

The system pairs a visible-light camera with a thermal camera, so it can see by day and by night. From altitude it can detect a target, then recognise, identify and track it. A laser pointer keeps continuous lock on moving targets.

02Autonomous system

The autopilot is a compact flight control computer, customized for full autonomy through vision-based object tracking. It manages every phase of flight: launch, climb, cruise, loiter and highly dynamic manoeuvres. It should be proven to operate in GNSS-denied environments, and it should include multiple redundancies to guard against failure.

The camera handles detection, recognition, identification and tracking using both visible and infrared imaging. This data travels to the ground control station (GCS) through the autopilot's integrated data link, where the live video can be viewed and monitored in the GCS software. To loiter over a target, the system flies a defined trajectory above it, using the target position calculated by vision-based geolocation algorithms.

03Propulsion system

Electric propulsion

The electric motor selected should provide optimum power for the UAV. This will allow for smokeless and very low acoustic signature. The accessories include compatable ESC and propeller.

04Ground control station (GCS)

The autopilot integrates with a joystick, a data link and ground control station (GCS) which comprises of a rugged hand-held display for running the GCS software. The software should have a specific protocols and parameters for target drones and includes every feature needed to run a loitering munition mission.

05Launcher

The tube launcher is man-portable and vehicle portable which can be assembled quickly. Its modular parts break down into loads small enough to carry, so a small crew can move it on foot and set it up in the field in a short time without special tools. The tube stores the folded airframe and protects it during transport, so the system stays ready to launch as soon as it's in position. Setup and maintenance are simple, which lets the team deploy, launch and relocate with minimal time on site.

06Munition system

The payload and its trigger mechanism were selected and integrated with support from munitions specialists. My work focused on the airframe side of that integration: providing a dedicated payload bay, defining the mounting and structural interfaces, and making sure the added mass kept the aircraft's centre of gravity and structural margins within limits. This let the payload be integrated without changing the airframe design.

Types of fuzes:

  • Point detonation
  • Proximity detonation
  • Barometric fuse.

Types of warhead:

  • Fuel air explosives (FAE)
  • High explosive anti-tank (HEAT)
  • High explosive fragmentation (HEF)

07Airframe

The initial airframe was of conventional design and was constructed out of wood with carbon composite sheeting and an epoxy finish. This model serves as a prototype flown at low altitudes and low speeds to fulfil the autonomous operation testing phase.

A more robust delta design was intended to be built completely using composites as a product for higher altitudes, high speeds and stealth operations.

Overview

Designed an amphibious UAV to collect water samples from flowing rivers — a stepped planing hull sized from seaplane practice, carrying a sampling probe below the after body.

Mission profile

The same airframe flown through its sortie: transit to the reach, powered approach along the channel, touchdown on the step, hull-borne drift while the probe draws the sample, then a planing take-off run and climb-out.

My role and highlights

The airframe uses three materials, each placed where it works best. The surfaces that meet the water, the hull bottom and the floats, are shaped from foam and laminated with a composite layup. This gives them a smooth, watertight skin and a clean hydrodynamic profile, so the aircraft can land on water, sit steady and take off again. The wing and fuselage are built on a wooden primary structure, which keeps the airframe light, stiff and easy to repair in the field. The E421 high wing was assessed at low Reynolds number in XFLR. The tail surfaces use a composite sandwich construction for high stiffness at minimum weight, keeping the control surfaces precise in both air and spray.

Fuselage sizing & CFD

The hull was sized from conventional seaplane practice: bottom flaring to divert spray, a step to break water drag and let the aircraft rotate, and a tapered afterbody for high-incidence take-off. Drag the controls to see the buoyancy and lift budgets move; the marked point is the flown configuration.

Overview

A VTOL surveillance UAV prototype, developed at the Indian Institute of Science (IISc), Bengaluru. It takes off and lands vertically, with no runway, then transitions to efficient fixed-wing cruise for longer surveillance coverage. I took the design from concept to flight. It's built on a lightweight wooden primary structure with composite hand lay-up, sized for the hover-to-cruise transition.

Mission profile

Internal structure

The VTOL is a hybrid quadplane. Four lift motors handle vertical takeoff and landing, and a pusher motor drives fixed-wing cruise. The airframe was designed as a lightweight skeleton that carries both sets of loads. The 2.5 m tapered high wing uses a rib-and-spar construction: evenly spaced ribs are threaded onto tubular spars, keeping it light while holding the Rhode 36 aerofoil profile. Two tubular booms run through the wing, parallel to the fuselage. Each boom carries two lift motor mounts, one ahead of the wing and one behind it, and the booms join at the rear to support the inverted V-tail. The fuselage is built from birch ribs under a monocoque skin. It houses the payload, autopilot and batteries, and provides the mount for the pusher motor.

The complete airframe weighs 3.35 kg out of an 8 kg maximum takeoff weight (fuselage 0.85 kg, wing 1.0 kg, tail and booms 1.5 kg). The structure was checked in a static structural FEA with a factor of safety of about 1.5, and von Mises stress and deflection were reviewed across the wing, booms and tail.
